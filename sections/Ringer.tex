\section{The \rnn{} algorithm}%
\label{sec:neuralringer}

The identification of electromagnetic objects at the \fastcalo{} stage of the HLT must efficiently reject the large hadronic background while operating under tight latency constraints.
The \rnn{} algorithm was developed to enhance this discrimination capability by exploiting the symmetry of electromagnetic showers in the calorimeter. Electrons and photons deposit most of their energy in a compact, centrally concentrated region, whereas hadronic jets exhibit broader, more irregular energy distributions extending into the hadronic calorimeter.

The idea behind the \rnn{} algorithm is to draw concentric rings perpendicular to the particle axis and estimate the energy deposited along each ring. This way, the algorithm summarizes the shower profile as a function of the radial distance from the shower axis, converting the spatially distributed calorimeter information into a compact, physics-motivated representation suitable for neural network classification. 


% The \rnn{} algorithm is relies on two main assumptions. First it is assumed that that the energy deposit of particles in the calorimeters have a conical symmetry around the position where the particle hits the detector. The other asumption is that the profile of the energy deposited in the calorimeter as a function of the distance to the particle is different for electrons or photons and QCD particle jets. 
% %Insteadof using electron shower-shape variables~\cite{TRIG-2018-05} to discriminate between electrons and background, the Neural-Ringer algorithm uses another construction to represent the shower information, referred to here as 'Rings'. These are transverse energy rings, built from sums of calorimeter-cell energies, around each candidate provided as an RoI by the L1 trigger. The distribution of those energy sums is used by a set of neural networks to distinguish electrons from background. This section describes the Neural-Ringer algorithm.

\subsection{Building the rings}\label{ssec:rnn_for_online_and_eletrons}

%The ATLAS sampling calorimeters provides a measurement of the energy deposited by the interacting particle at different depths.
%
%The implementation of the rings in the ATLAS calorimeters rely on the granularity of each calorimeter at each layer. 

The implementation of the \rnn{} defines rings at each layer of both EM and HAD calorimeters. 
In the ATLAS calorimeter, the rings are built with a rectangular shape to match the grid layout of the calorimeter cells. 
Since this algorithm was designed to be used in the \fastcalo{} step of the Trigger reconstruction, all the rings are confined within the area defined by the RoI and centered around the cell with the highest energy deposited closest to the L1 RoI position.
As a general rule, a ring of size $n$ in a given layer of the calorimeter consists of the cells lying in the border of an $n \times n$ cell area. 

%Since the Neural-Ringer algorithm was designed to operate online in software at the \fastcalo{} stage, approximations were made. Specifically, rectangular rings are constructed to match the grid layout of the calorimeter cells, and the ring coverage is bounded by the RoI's area. The granularity changes in different regions of the detector are neglected in order to design an algorithm that uses a cell grid defined as a number of cells in the ($\eta$,$\phi$) directions so that the $\eta\times\phi$ region where the rings are built is not exactly constant along the full detector. This effect is mostly relevant for the EM1 layer where the $\eta$-granularity used by the algorithm is 0.003 for  $0<|\eta|<1.81$, 0.004 for $1.81<|\eta|<2.01$ and 0.006 for $2.01<|\eta|<2.37$.




% The algorithm's RoI covers a calorimeter region of $0.4\times0.4$ in the $\eta\times\phi$ plane, centred on the L1-provided RoI. 
% The algorithm starts at the EM2, where the $\eta\times\phi$ position of the cell with the highest \ET in the RoI is taken as the centre of the cascade interaction in the calorimeter. This initial seed-cell position is propagated to other calorimeter layers in order to define the axis of the rings in those layers. Then, for each layer, the algorithm refines the seed position by searching for the most energetic cell inside a reconstruction window of $0.1\times0.1$ in the $\eta\times\phi$ plane centred on the EM2 initial seed, and the energy deposition of an incoming particle is extracted by building concentric rings of cells, simply referred to as \enquote{rings}.

The actual definition of the rings is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:calo_rings}. In the EM calorimeter, rings are centered around the most energetic cell at each layer, while in the
hadronic calorimeter, the $\eta \times \phi$ position of the most energetic cell
in the second layer of the EM calorimeter is used to determine the central ring.
%The refined seed ($c_{\text{hot},l}$) is used as the starting point to build the rings. A ring $R_{n,l}$ contains all $c_{n,l}$ cells in calorimeter layer $l$ which are $n$ cells away from the refined seed (\figurename~\ref{fig:calo_rings} shows the parameters used for each layer.). 
Formally, the cells that belong to a given ring of size $n$ on a given layer $l$ is given by Equation \ref{eq:ring_idx} where $\eta_{i,l}$, $\phi_{i,l}$ represent the position of the cell $i$ at detector layer $l$, the subindex ``hot'' refers to the central cell (first ring) from a given layer; 
%and $\eta_{\text{hot},l}$, $\phi_{\text{hot},l}$ are respectively the $c_{i,l}$ and $c_{\text{hot},l}$
%cells' positions in $\eta$ and $\phi$ plane; 
$h_{\eta,l}$  and $h_{\phi,l}$ are the sizes in $\eta$ and $\phi$ of the cell at the layer $l$ ; $\Theta_{\text{RoI},l}$ is the set of cells
in the $l^{\text{th}}$ layer which are within the RoI ; and $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ is the \textit{round} function.
\tablename~\ref{tab:ring_alg_parameters} shows the number of rings computed at each layer and the respective granularity.


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ring_idx}
R_{n,l} = \left\{c_{n,l} \mid n = \left\lfloor \max{\left( 
\frac{| \eta_{i,l} - \eta_{\text{hot},l} |}{h_{\eta,l}}, 
\frac{| \phi_{i,l} - \phi_{\text{hot},l} |}{h_{\phi,l}} 
\right)} \right\rceil, 
\forall c_{i,l} \in
\Theta_{\text{RoI},l}
\right\},
\end{equation}

For every layer of the EM and HAD calorimeters, the transverse energy deposited in the cells of each ring is summed. % to form a one-dimensional descriptor of the shower.
Across all layers, a total of 100 ring transverse energies are computed, ordered from the innermost EM region to the outermost hadronic layer, and stored in a single vector. Each index in that vector identifies a ring. 
The energy normalization of the rings is performed as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:ring_norm})
where $r_k$ or $r_i$ is the sum of transverse energy of the cells in the ring with index $k$ or $i$, and $r^\prime_{k}$ is the normalized ring energy value with index $k$.
The absolute value is used in the denominator to handle extreme cases where the total energy sum is negative. In this way, the consistency of positive or negative values is maintained after normalization. This procedure was initially proposed and examined in Ref.~\cite{1995_seixas_ringer} as a way of preserving the shower energy profile as fractions of the total energy. Building rings in this way summarizes the information of thousands of calorimeter cells into just 100 variables, while preserving both the longitudinal and lateral structure of the energy deposition.

\begin{equation}
  r^\prime_{k} = \frac{r_{k}}{| \sum\limits_{i=1}^{100} r_i
  |}, \;\;\;
    \forall \; k\in\{1,\dots,100\},
\label{eq:ring_norm}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Motivation for using a set of neural networks}\label{ssec:nn_set}

The detector response and discrimination power between electrons and QCD jets vary with the candidate $E_T$ and its $\eta$ position, due to the different granularities of the calorimeters at different pseudorapidities. Therefore, a set of multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) trained on different regions of $E_T \times |\eta|$ is used to classify the normalized vector of ring energies. 
% For calorimetry, the use of a set of models, each specific to a particular phase-space region, mitigates fluctuations in the variables' profiles
% due to differences in shower energy and detector response.
% A large contribution comes from discrete detector-granularity changes in the phase space. Other important factors 
% influencing such profiles are the
% amount of the detector material as a function of \abseta{} and the dependence of the
% underlying shower-development processes on the incoming
% particle's energy. Although the latter changes are mostly continuous, it is also
% possible to approach the problem via space discretization.
Splitting the training into multiple small models enables higher discrimination power and efficient in terms of computing resources compared to a single model that is inclusive over all $E_T$ and $|\eta|$ regions.

Limiting the memory required for tuning the models was of particular interest in order to benefit from low-memory processing nodes, as these were the ones mostly available in the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid~\cite{2015_lcg_tdr} during the model developments process effort.\@ In addition, the decomposition of the problem can also lead to a reduction in training time~\cite{Polikar2006}, as observed heuristically

To train the models, electrons from the signal sample are selected using the \tnp method described in \cite{TRIG-2018-05}, in which one electron (the tag) is used to trigger the event and is requested to satisfy tight identification and isolation requirements offline. A second electron (the probe) is required to be in the acceptance of the EM Calo, requiring only that the invariant mass of the Tag and Probe system is compatible with the Z boson mass window. %This makes the probe almost unbiased with respect to offline identification and completely unbiased with respect to the trigger selection.

The model parameters were optimized using events selected from simulated \Zee datasets. The network architecture consists of a single fully connected hidden layer with 5 to 20 hidden neurons, an input layer with 100 nodes (one for each ring), and an output layer with a single neuron.
The activation function for both the hidden and output neurons is the hyperbolic tangent, which is commonly used in MLP designs~\cite{haykin_2008}.


To assess the statistical fluctuations in the efficiency measurements with respect to the model parameters obtained from the training procedure, the stratified $k$-fold cross-validation method~\cite{haykin_2008} with $k=10$ was employed. The stratified $k$-fold is one of the most common cross-validation techniques and consists of partitioning the dataset into $k$ disjoint subsets, with each model being tested on the $i^\text{th}$ subset after being trained on the other $k-1$ subsets. The dataset stratification follows the empirical order of event selection, allowing for straightforward job parallelization; therefore, no random permutation is employed. 

For each cross-validation sort and working point, 100 networks, initialized as in~\cite{initnw}, are optimized by back-propagating the mean squared error through the RPROP algorithm~\cite{rprop}. This repeated model initialization aims to avoid suboptimal solutions that may arise from gradient descent algorithms.
For each training procedure, only three models out of the 100 are retained: two for retrieving the best efficiency when fixing the sensitivity or false positive probabilities to the reference \fastcalo{} efficiency, and another that gives the maximum $SP$ value. 

The maximum SP is a value that optimizes the balance between signal detection probability and the probability of fake acceptance in the ROC curve (i.e., the knee of the curve) and is defined as:

\begin{equation*}
SP = \sqrt{\sqrt{P_\text{D}(1-F_\text{A})}\cdot\frac{P_\text{D} + (1-F_\text{A})}{2}}
\end{equation*}


\noindent where $P_\text{D}$ is the signal detection probability (or sensitivity) and $F_\text{A}$ is the probability of a fake signal (false positive). 
An early stopping technique~\cite{haykin_2008} is employed to prevent overtraining. The selection of the optimal iteration and operating model is based on a heuristic approach (multi-stop), as described in~\cite{Goodfellow2016}.

After the training stage, particle identification is done by applying a threshold requirement to the one-dimensional output node of the MLP associated with the \ eta region of a given electron candidate. 
As in the HLT likelihood \cite{aaboud2019electron} algorithm, the threshold value used in the \rnn algorithm should be computed as a linear function of \avgmu{}. This allows threshold adjustment as \avgmu{} changes, maintaining the detection rate approximately constant. However, a non-linear behaviour was observed near the asymptote of the output activation function (see Figure~\ref{fig:nn_correction_with_tansig}) when using the \enquote{medium} or \enquote{tight} operating point. This was caused by the nonlinear activation function in the output neuron, and it can be avoided by replacing the hyperbolic tangent activation function, only in the output neuron, with a linear function (Figure~\ref{fig:nn_correction_without_tansig}). All neurons in the hidden layer retained the hyperbolic tangent activation function, preserving the architecture used during training. This simple strategy ensures a linear relation of the targeted\footnote{same signal detection probability as the online precision electron LH algorithm, increased by a factor of 50\% for each of the four trigger operation points.} signal efficiency with respect to the online pileup estimator, similar to what was used in \cite{aaboud2019electron}. 
To account for Monte Carlo and collision data differences (see Figure~\ref{fig:nn_mc15_vs_data16}), the parameters of the linear threshold correction were derived using 2016 collision data and an upper bound of $\avgmu=40$ pileup interactions (i.e.\ the corrected threshold value is constant for pileup values above this upper bound).
